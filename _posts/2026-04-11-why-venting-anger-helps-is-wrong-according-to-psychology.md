Ever felt that surge of anger, that boiling frustration, and been told, "Just let it out! Vent, scream, punch a pillow!"? It's a common piece of advice, almost a societal ritual for dealing with rage. For years, the idea that "catharsis" – purging negative emotions through expression – was the healthy way to cope with anger reigned supreme.

You might even remember feeling a momentary sense of relief after a good rant. But here's the thing: that relief is often a mirage, and the long-term effects of habitual venting can actually make your anger worse. Turns out, what feels intuitive often isn't what's best for our minds, a truth both modern psychology and ancient Stoic wisdom have been telling us for centuries.

## The Myth of Anger Catharsis: Why Venting Doesn't Work

Many of us grew up believing that anger is like a pressure cooker. If you don't release the steam, it'll explode. So, we're encouraged to yell, complain, or lash out. The logic seems sound: get it out of your system, and you'll feel better.

But decades of psychological research paint a very different picture. Instead of diminishing anger, expressing it aggressively often fuels it. It's like pouring gasoline on a fire, not extinguishing it.

### The Psychological Rebound Effect of Venting

When you vent, you're not just getting rid of anger; you're often rehearsing it. Each time you replay the upsetting event, recount the injustice, or re-feel the outrage, you reinforce those neural pathways associated with anger. Your brain gets better at being angry.

Studies have shown that people who frequently vent their anger tend to remain angrier, longer, and become more prone to future outbursts. It keeps you stuck in a loop of negative emotion, rather than helping you move past it.

## Stoicism on Anger: Master Your Inner World

Long before modern psychology had fMRI machines, the Stoics understood the deceptive nature of anger. They viewed anger not as an external force acting upon us, but as an internal judgment, a choice we make about how to interpret events.

Seneca, one of the most prominent Stoic philosophers, dedicated an entire treatise to anger. He saw it as a temporary madness, destructive to both the individual and society. He firmly believed that expressing anger, far from resolving it, only strengthens its grip.

> "The best way to avenge yourself is not to be like him." – Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*

This timeless wisdom echoes what psychologists now confirm: focusing on the offense, or the offender, keeps you tied to the very thing that made you angry. It prevents you from taking back control of your inner peace.
